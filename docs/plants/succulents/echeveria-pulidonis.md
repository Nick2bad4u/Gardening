## Names and identification

| Kind            | Name                                                                    |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Best working ID | _Echeveria pulidonis_ E.Walther, or a hybrid derived from it            |
| Common name     | Pulido's echeveria                                                      |
| Genus history   | _Echeveria_ honors Mexican botanical artist Atanasio Echeverría y Godoy |

The collection rosette has the useful field marks: powdery blue-gray leaves,
pointed tips, and a fine red line around the margins. Many commercial hybrids
inherit exactly that combination, so the ID should remain probable until a
flower stalk shows its branching, bracts, and flower form.

## Origin, form, and flowers

Kew accepts _E. pulidonis_ as a Mexican species first described by Edward
Walther in 1972. Its native range includes Hidalgo, Puebla, and Veracruz, where
it grows in seasonally dry shrubland. Kew's automated conservation model flags
possible threat but with low confidence; that is a prediction, not a formal
Red List assessment.

The species forms a compact stemless-looking rosette roughly 12 cm across,
eventually making offsets. Mature plants send up arching stalks carrying
nodding, urn-shaped yellow flowers. The flower color is particularly useful:
many red-edged _Echeveria_ lookalikes have red, orange, or bicolored flowers.

The pale coating is epicuticular wax, commonly called farina. It reflects light
and reduces water loss. Fingerprints remove it from old tissue, so handle the
pot or lowest leaves rather than polishing the rosette.

## Care in the shared planter

| Topic                     | Practical approach                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Grow-light position       | Brightest part of the succulent planter. Start around 10–18 mol/m²/day DLI, about 230–420 µmol/m²/s over 12 hours.                                                            |
| Evidence behind the range | A 2026 study on other _Echeveria_ cultivars found 12.8 mol/m²/day produced more compact, better-performing plants than 4.8; it is guidance, not a species-specific threshold. |
| Water                     | Soak the shared root zone only after it has dried. Keep water out of the rosette in cool, still conditions.                                                                   |
| Competition               | Prevent the elephant bush and kalanchoes from shading or physically crowding the rosette.                                                                                     |
| Feeding                   | Light dilute feeding in active growth. Excess fertilizer plus weak light opens the rosette.                                                                                   |

Red margins can intensify with useful light and normal mild stress. That is not
the same as a bleached tan patch, translucent tissue, or a sharply scorched leaf
surface. Judge the center: closely stacked new leaves indicate adequate light;
a raised center and widening gaps indicate stretch.

## Propagation and watch points

- Offsets are the easiest true-to-type propagation. Stem and intact leaf
  cuttings can also root after the wound dries, although success varies by
  hybrid.
- Lower leaves are a normal water reserve and may be reabsorbed one at a time.
  Several translucent leaves at once suggest excess moisture.
- Mealybugs hide at leaf bases; aphids may appear on flower stalks.
- Do not repeatedly add small sips because the echeveria looks less plump than
  its woody planter companions. A shared soak-and-dry cycle is safer.
- If it repeatedly stretches while the others thrive, raising its side of the
  planter or separating it is simpler than overlighting every plant.
