# Variegated moon cactus

- Inventory: Starter-07 — _Gymnocalycium mihanovichii_
- Label ID: `A1`
- Identification: **labeled; own-root variegated plant**
- Photo archive: [licensed reference images](../../../assets/plants/gymnocalycium-mihanovichii-variegated/)

## Names and identity

| Kind                    | Name                                                                        |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accepted botanical name | _Gymnocalycium mihanovichii_ (Frič ex Gürke) Britton & Rose                 |
| Common and trade names  | Moon cactus, chin cactus, plaid cactus, Hibotan                             |
| Collection form         | Variegated, but still visibly green and apparently growing on its own roots |
| Name history            | The species honors shipping magnate and plant patron Nicolás Mihanovich     |

Most neon “moon cacti” in stores are chlorophyll-free colored tops grafted onto
a green rootstock. This plant is different: its green sectors appear sufficient
to support an own-root plant. The orange, yellow, and bronze areas are reduced
chlorophyll, not automatically sunburn or rot.

_Gymnocalycium_ means “naked calyx.” The flower tube and outer floral parts lack
the hairs and spines seen on many other cactus genera. That is a useful clue
when the plant eventually blooms.

## Origin and horticultural history

The wild species comes from Paraguay and northeastern Argentina, including
seasonally dry Chaco vegetation on open, rocky or sandy ground. It is a small,
flattened cactus with broad ribs divided into chin-like bumps beneath the
areoles. Pale greenish-white to pink flowers arise near the crown.

The spectacular colored forms are a human selection story. Arkansas Cooperative
Extension summarizes the Japanese history: Eiji Watanabe imported seed in 1937,
raised thousands of seedlings by 1940, found two reddish mutants, and continued
selection through grafting. Completely chlorophyll-free descendants survive
only on a photosynthesizing stock. Partly green _Hibotan-nishiki_ forms can
remain on their own roots, which is the more relevant comparison for this
collection plant.

Kew accepts _G. mihanovichii_ and traces the combination to Britton and Rose in 1922. The species was originally described in _Echinocactus_.

## Care in this collection

| Topic               | Practical approach                                                                                         |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Grow-light position | Gentler edge or raised side zone. Start around 6–12 mol/m²/day DLI, about 140–280 µmol/m²/s over 12 hours. |
| Why gentler         | Reduced-green tissue has less photosynthetic capacity and often burns before fully green high-light cacti. |
| Water               | Soak, drain, and allow a full dry-down. Do not keep it wet to compensate for slow growth.                  |
| Pot and mix         | The current small drained pot is appropriate. Keep the colored lower body visible above the top dressing.  |
| Temperature         | Warm growing conditions and a bright, drier winter suit it better than cold wet treatment.                 |
| Feeding             | Very dilute feeding during active growth; variegation makes slower growth normal.                          |

Use the green portions as the best health indicator. Stable colored panels with
firm tissue are normal. A newly bleached, papery patch on the lamp-facing side
is sun stress; a spreading translucent or soft patch is a water/rot concern.

## Propagation and watch points

- Green-containing offsets may root on their own. An offset with no green
  tissue needs a compatible grafting stock to survive.
- Seedlings will not reliably reproduce the exact variegation pattern.
- Spider mites can scar the relatively bare skin; inspect the recessed crown
  and rib valleys.
- Do not confuse normal rib “chins” and color boundaries with collapse.
  Firmness and change over time matter more than one still photo.
- Keep a dated overhead photo. Variegated patterns make slow growth surprisingly
  easy to track.
