# Rainbow hedgehog cactus

- Inventory: Starter-03 — _Echinocereus rigidissimus_ subsp. _rubispinus_
- Label ID: `C2`
- Visual description: Compact cylinder covered in tight bands of pink, red, and white comb-like spines.
- Interesting fact: Its vivid body color comes mostly from the spines; mature plants can add large magenta flowers near the crown.

## Names and identity

| Kind                             | Name                                                                           |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Accepted botanical name          | _Echinocereus rigidissimus_ subsp. _rubispinus_ (G.Frank & A.B.Lau) N.P.Taylor |
| Common and trade names           | Rainbow hedgehog cactus, ruby rainbow cactus                                   |
| Earlier rank seen in collections | _Echinocereus rigidissimus_ var. _rubispinus_                                  |
| Name clue                        | _rubispinus_ means ruby- or red-spined                                         |

The user-supplied “rubrispinus” was close, but the accepted epithet is
_rubispinus_. New radial spines emerge ruby, pink, or magenta and fade as they
age, producing horizontal color bands as the stem grows. There are normally no
large projecting central spines; the dense comb of radials hugs the body.

## Origin and life cycle

The subspecies is native to northwestern Mexico, especially Chihuahua, where it
occupies sunny, rocky slopes in dry upland habitat. It is usually a solitary
column rather than a freely branching clump. Dense spines shade the epidermis
but still allow photosynthesis through the gaps.

Large bright magenta-pink flowers with a conspicuously pale or white throat
appear around the upper portion of a sufficiently mature stem, commonly in
spring. Each flower can rival the stem diameter. The striking spine color made
the subspecies a collector favorite, but cultivated color varies with genetics,
age, season, and light; a greenish lower band is not automatically a problem.

The form was originally described at varietal rank by Gerhard Frank and Alfred
Lau. Nigel Taylor published the subspecies combination accepted by Kew in 1997.

## Care in this collection

| Topic               | Practical approach                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Grow-light position | One of the higher-light plants. After a gradual ramp, try roughly 14–24 mol/m²/day DLI, or 325–555 µmol/m²/s averaged over 12 hours. |
| Outdoor light       | Increasing direct sun is useful, but a greenhouse-grown stem can burn before its spine cover and epidermis adapt.                    |
| Water               | Thorough watering followed by a complete dry-down in warm growth. Keep dry or nearly dry during cool winter rest.                    |
| Mix                 | Favor a very open mineral fraction around the roots. Never let the tray act as a reservoir.                                          |
| Flowering support   | Strong seasonal light and a cool, dry winter rest are more useful than heavy fertilizer.                                             |

The target range is a starting point, not a demand to run the AW200SE at full
power. The best signal is dense, even, colorful new spination on a stem that
keeps its diameter. Pale corky patches on the lamp-facing side are a reason to
back off.

## Propagation and watch points

- Seed is the usual propagation method; solitary plants offer few offsets.
- This species is notably unforgiving of a cold, wet root ball. Cold tolerance
  reported for dry established plants should not be copied to a recently
  watered 4-inch pot.
- Scale insects can be hard to see through dense spines. Inspect the crown with
  a bright light rather than pushing fingers into it.
- The root system does not need a huge pot. Repot when roots occupy the current
  container or the mix no longer drains freely.
